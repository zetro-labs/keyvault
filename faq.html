Getting started

What KeyVault is, how your vault is protected, and the questions everyone asks first.

KeyVault is a password manager by Zetro Labs for Android. It keeps your passwords, passcodes, and passkeys in a single encrypted vault on your phone, locked behind one master password — so you only ever have to remember one password, and every other login can be long, unique, and impossible to guess.

KeyVault also checks the health of your saved passwords, alerts you when a password has appeared in a known data breach, generates strong new passwords, passphrases, and PINs, and can act as your phone's autofill service and passkey provider.

KeyVault was created by Zetro Labs. It is an exclusive product of Zetro Labs, and Zetro Labs retains all rights and authority over the app. Any legal issues arising in connection with KeyVault are subject to the jurisdiction of the Indian judiciary (the courts of India). The same statements appear in the Privacy Policy and Terms & Conditions tabs above.

Yes — your vault stays on your device. Zetro Labs has no servers and no KeyVault accounts, and we never receive, see, or store your passwords. Your entries are encrypted on your phone with a key derived from your master password.

The only exceptions are features you choose to use: an encrypted backup you export yourself, an automatic backup saved on your phone, or an encrypted backup in your own Google Drive. Even then, the backup is encrypted before it leaves the vault, so nobody — including Google and Zetro Labs — can read its contents without the password.

The master password is the one password that unlocks your vault. It is used to derive the encryption key that protects everything inside; KeyVault itself never stores the master password, and it never leaves your phone.

Choose one that is long and unique — a phrase of several unrelated words works well — and never reuse it anywhere else. Because it protects everything, losing it is serious: see the next question.

Zetro Labs cannot recover it for you — by design. There is no account, no server copy, and no back door: that is exactly what keeps your vault safe from everyone else, too.

Your one way back in is your emergency recovery kit (Settings → Security → Emergency access), which lets you regain access and set a new master password. If you never saved a recovery kit and you forget the master password, the vault cannot be opened and the only option is to erase it (Settings → Danger zone → Delete everything) and start fresh. Set up your recovery kit now, while you still can — it takes a minute.

The recovery kit is a 256-bit recovery key, shown once as a 58-character code together with a QR code. It is a separate wrap of your vault's encryption key, independent of your master password — so it keeps working even if you change your master password, and a copy of the recovery wrap travels inside every backup and sync payload.

Create it in Settings → Security → Emergency access, then store it somewhere safe and offline — printed on paper in a secure place is ideal. A checksum in the code catches typing mistakes when you enter it. If you ever get locked out, choose the recovery option on the lock screen, enter or scan the kit, and you'll be asked to set a new master password.

Treat the kit like your master password: anyone who has it can unlock your vault.

A passkey is the modern replacement for a password — a cryptographic key pair created for one website or app. When you create a passkey, two matching halves are made: the website keeps the public half, and the private half is stored encrypted in your vault. Signing in works like a signature check: the site sends a one-time challenge, KeyVault signs it with the private half after you approve with your fingerprint or device unlock, and the site verifies the signature against the public half. The private key itself is never sent anywhere, and your fingerprint never leaves your phone — it only approves the signing. Because each passkey is bound to the exact site it was created for, a look-alike phishing site gets nothing it can use — there is no password to type, steal, or reuse. KeyVault stores passkeys and acts as your phone's passkey provider (Android 14 or later): when a site offers 'Create a passkey', choose KeyVault and the passkey is saved straight into your vault, protected and backed up like everything else in it. See Upgrade to passkey in the Security tab for turning existing passwords into passkeys.

Two-factor authentication (2FA) adds a second proof at sign-in: your password — something you know — plus a code from something you have: this phone. The most common kind is TOTP, short for Time-based One-Time Password. When you set it up, the website and KeyVault share a secret key. From then on, both sides work out the same code on their own, from that secret and the current time — the code changes every 30 seconds, and no connection between KeyVault and the website is needed for the codes to match. A code is useless seconds later, and unlike SMS codes, nothing travels over the phone network where it could be intercepted. When a site shows you a setup QR code or setup key, save it in the entry's 2FA/TOTP secret field: tap Scan to scan the QR code, or type or paste the key — a pasted otpauth:// link works too. KeyVault reads the code length, timing and algorithm from the link, so services that use non-standard settings still produce the right codes. The secret is stored encrypted inside your vault, exactly like the password, and the codes are computed on your phone, fully offline — nothing about 2FA ever leaves your device.

Tap the 2FA codes filter at the top of the Vault tab. The list narrows to the entries that have a 2FA secret, and each row shows its current code with the seconds left before it changes — the codes refresh on screen as you watch. Tap a row to copy its code; the clipboard clears automatically, just like copied passwords. An entry's detail view shows its live code too. Because the codes are time-based, keep your phone's date and time set to automatic — if the clock is off, the codes will not match what the website expects.

Tap any row to open its detail view. From there you can view the saved details (with an eye toggle to reveal or hide the secret), copy a username or password, edit the entry, mark it as a favorite, or delete it. Copied secrets are cleared from the clipboard automatically after the time you set in Settings.

Security tab

Your security score, four health checks, and the fixes that keep your vault strong.

The ring at the top of the Security tab shows a score out of 100 summarising the health of your saved passwords — reduced by weak passwords and reused passwords. Passwords found in breaches are flagged separately under Breach alerts and don't change the score. Fix the items under Needs attention and the score climbs. A perfect 100 still shows the full ring, so you can always see where you stand.

KeyVault analyses the strength of every saved password on your device — length, variety of characters, and how guessable it is — and counts the ones that would be easy for an attacker to crack. Weak passwords appear under Needs attention; change them on the website (the Generator can make a strong replacement), then update the entry in KeyVault.

It counts passwords that are identical across two or more entries. Reuse is dangerous: if one site leaks, attackers try that same password everywhere else ("credential stuffing"). Give every account its own password — the Generator makes this painless — and this count drops to zero.

Tap Breach alerts to check your passwords against Have I Been Pwned (HIBP), a well-known database of passwords exposed in public data breaches. It uses a privacy-preserving method called k-anonymity:

  1. Your password is hashed (SHA-1) on your phone.
  2. Only the first 5 characters of that hash are sent to HIBP — never your password, and never the full hash.
  3. HIBP returns every known hash starting with those 5 characters, and your phone checks locally whether yours is among them.

Because only a 5-character prefix ever leaves the device, HIBP never learns your passwords. The check needs an internet connection, since it talks to HIBP online. Prefer hands-off? Set Breach checks to Automatic in Settings → Security and KeyVault checks on unlock, at most once a day — quietly, unless it finds something.

Change that password on the website as soon as you can, update the entry in KeyVault, and anywhere else you reused it. The entry's breach history is kept so you can see it was flagged.

KeyVault cannot tell you which company leaked it — nobody can from this data. HIBP's password database reports only how many times a password has appeared across all known breaches (counts only, by design); it doesn't record which breach a given password came from.

Needs attention lists the individual entries behind the check counts — each weak or reused password — with a reason. Breached passwords are flagged separately, with a warning at the top of the entry's detail view. Tapping Fix opens that entry's detail view so you can act on it: generate a strong replacement in the Generator, change it on the website, and save the new one. Once fixed, the entry drops off the list and your score improves.

Many websites now let you replace a password with a passkey — faster and far more phishing-resistant. Upgrade to passkey guides you through doing exactly that, in three screens:

  1. The list — your saved logins whose websites are known to support passkeys.
  2. The detail screen — three steps: open the site, create a passkey in the site's own settings choosing KeyVault as the provider, then return to KeyVault.
  3. Confirmation — when you come back and unlock, KeyVault checks whether a new passkey was saved and shows a "Passkey saved" confirmation.

The list carries a note — "Passkey support data as of 19/10/2026" — because websites add passkey support over time; the list is refreshed with app updates.

When you tap Open site, KeyVault remembers the pending upgrade on the device. When you return to KeyVault and unlock it, KeyVault checks the vault for a new passkey for that site: if one is found you get the "Passkey saved" confirmation; if not, it asks "Did you create a passkey?" — tap Yes or No.

The pending check survives the app being closed or swiped away, because it's stored on the phone rather than held in memory. To avoid confusion later, it expires after one hour: if you come back after that, it's quietly discarded with no pop-up.

When a passkey is saved, you can keep the old password entry as a fallback in case a site or device doesn't offer the passkey at sign-in. If you try to turn Keep password as backup off, KeyVault first warns: "Before turning off backup, ensure that the passkey is registered in KeyVault or another app" — with Cancel and Continue options — so you never remove your only way into an account.

The upgrade list itself works fully offline — it's local data. Only the Open site step needs the internet, because it opens the website in your browser.

Generator tab

Strong passwords, memorable passphrases, and PINs — made for you, on the spot.

The segmented control at the top of the Generator switches between three kinds of secret:

  • Password — a random mix of letters, numbers, and symbols. Best for logins KeyVault will remember and fill for you.
  • Passphrase — a chain of random words, each picked at random from a 256-word list. Easier to read, type, and memorise — 8 words rates strong and 10 or more very strong — so it's a good choice for your master password or anything you must type by hand.
  • PIN — random digits only, for places that accept nothing else.

The slider sets the size of what you're generating — characters for a password, words for a passphrase, digits for a PIN. Longer is stronger: the minimums are 6 characters, 6 words, and 4 digits. Drag the slider and the generated value and its strength update to match.

In Password mode, these switches control which character sets are included: capital letters, digits, and symbols like ! # $ %. Turn one off if a website forbids it (some sites reject symbols); leave them on for maximum strength. Lowercase letters are always included.

The meter estimates how hard the current result would be to guess or crack, based on its length and variety. If it reads weak, drag the length slider higher or switch more character sets on — the meter updates live as you adjust.

  • Copy — puts the generated value on the clipboard so you can paste it into a sign-up or password-change form. It is wiped automatically after the delay set in Settings → Clear clipboard after.
  • Regenerate — throws the current result away and makes a fresh one with the same settings. Tap it as often as you like.
  • Save to vault — opens the Add entry form with the generated value already filled in as the password, so you can name it and save it as a new vault entry — a strong password never has to exist only on your clipboard.

Generation happens entirely on your phone using your device's secure randomness, the moment you open the tab or tap Regenerate. Nothing is sent anywhere — the Generator works fully offline, even in airplane mode.

Settings → Security → Change master password. You'll enter your current master password, then the new one; the vault is re-locked to the new password. Your emergency recovery kit remains valid afterwards, and changing the master password is also the final step when you recover access with the kit.

With Fingerprint unlock on, you can open KeyVault with your fingerprint instead of typing the master password every time. Whenever you copy a password — from an entry or the Generator — it sits on Android's clipboard, where other apps could read it. The switch reflects the real system state, so it always shows whether autofill is genuinely active.

Backup on this phone

Export backup writes your whole vault to a single encrypted backup file and asks you where to save it. The file is protected by your master password — the same encryption as the vault itself — so it can't be opened without that password. There is no separate backup password to set or remember. Keep the file somewhere safe — it's the thing you'd restore from if your phone is lost or replaced.

Auto backup saves an up-to-date local backup automatically — choose Off, Daily, or Weekly. Backups are stored in the Download/KeyVault folder on your phone, and if the vault is ever empty at setup (for example on a replacement phone where the folder came across), KeyVault can restore from the newest of those backups — you just enter its master password.

Import backup restores your vault from the most recent backup in the Download/KeyVault folder. There is no file to pick — the latest backup is found automatically. You enter the master password that backup was made with, and your entries are restored. Use it when moving to a new phone or recovering after a reinstall.

Import from Chrome brings in a CSV file exported from Chrome / Google Password Manager:

  1. In Chrome or Google Password Manager, export your passwords to a CSV file and get it onto your phone.
  2. In KeyVault, tap Import from Chrome and pick the CSV file.
  3. KeyVault reads the file and adds the entries, skipping ones you already have.

Important: a Chrome CSV export is not encrypted — anyone who opens it can read every password. Delete the CSV file as soon as the import finishes, and don't send it anywhere.

2.Information stored on your device

Everything you put into KeyVault stays in the app's private storage on your phone, encrypted at rest with a key derived from your master password:

  • Security information computed locally, such as password-strength results and per-entry breach history.

Your master password is never stored — in the app, on the device, or anywhere else — and it is never transmitted. When the app is sent to the background, unlocked vault data is cleared from memory, and screenshots of the app are blocked by the system while the vault is in use.

3.Information we collect

None. We do not collect, receive, sell, rent, or share any personal information. KeyVault has:

Because we hold no data about you, we also cannot recover your master password or your vault if you lose them — see Section 7.

4.Offline by default

KeyVault works fully offline for everyday use, and makes no network connections at all unless you use a feature that needs one. The internet is used only for the features in Section 5, each of which you trigger yourself.

5.Optional online features — what leaves your device, and only when you ask

(a) Breach alerts — Have I Been Pwned (HIBP). When you run a breach check, each password is hashed (SHA-1) on your device and only the first 5 characters of the hash are sent to HIBP over a secure connection, using the k-anonymity model. Your passwords and full hashes never leave the device. HIBP returns candidate matches, which your device compares locally. HIBP's handling of the prefix it receives is governed by HIBP's own privacy policy.

(b) Google Drive backup and sync. If you link your Google account, KeyVault uploads a backup of your vault to the app-data folder of your own Google Drive. The backup is encrypted on your device before upload; neither Google nor Zetro Labs can decrypt it. KeyVault cannot access any other files in your Drive. You can unlink the account at any time, and delete the backup from your Drive yourself. Google's processing of your account and Drive data is governed by Google's Privacy Policy.

(c) Opening websites. If an entry has a website saved, you can open it in your browser from the entry's detail view, and during a passkey upgrade KeyVault opens a website in your browser at your request. Whatever you do on that website is between you and the website, under its own privacy policy.

(d) Website icons in the item picker. When you open the Website picker on the Add Item screen, the app fetches each listed site's icon (favicon) from Google's favicon service. The request contains only the site's domain — no vault data and no personal information is sent. Icons for apps installed on your phone are read locally on the device and never leave it.

No other network communication takes place. In particular, KeyVault never contacts Zetro Labs.

6.Backups on your device

Exported and automatic backups saved on your phone (for example in Download/KeyVault) are encrypted files protected by your backup password. They are files in your own storage: you control where they are kept, who they are shared with, and when they are deleted. Anyone with both the file and its password could restore the vault, so keep them somewhere safe.

7.Permissions and system roles

  • Internet — used only for the features in Section 5.

  • Autofill service & credential provider — system roles you may enable in Android settings so KeyVault can fill logins and store passkeys. Credentials are offered only after you unlock and approve.

Your data exists only where you put it: on your device, in backup files you exported, and (if you linked it) in your own Google Drive. Delete individual entries at any time; use Settings → Danger zone → Delete everything to erase everything on the device; uninstalling the app removes its on-device data. Backup files and Drive backups persist until you delete them, since they are yours.
